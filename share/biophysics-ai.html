From signals

Biophysics explains how living systems work; AI helps find patterns and build models. The strongest work joins both with experiments, careful validation, and domain expertise.

01 / Where it shows up

Six real-world intersections

Mechanism + measurement + computation

Images

Brain imaging

AI can help analyze MRI and other scans, segment structures, and quantify patterns for research or clinical workflows. [1]

Signals

Brain–computer interfaces

Signal processing and ML decode task-specific brain activity to control a cursor, device, or communication system. Results depend on the person, task, and setup. [2]

Molecules

Protein folding + drug discovery

Structure models can prioritize targets, binding questions, and experiments. A predicted structure is a hypothesis, not proof of binding, efficacy, or a therapy. [3]

Physiology

ECG

Models can detect waveform patterns that merit review, including patterns studied for atrial fibrillation. They do not replace clinical interpretation or establish treatment. [4, 5]

Neurons

Computational neuroscience

Equations and simulations test how ion channels, cells, and circuits could produce observed activity. A simulation is a model output, not patient evidence. [6, 7]

Tissues

Cancer biophysics

Mechanics, transport, and cell signaling help describe how tumors interact with their microenvironment; computation can quantify patterns and test research hypotheses. [8]

EEG is not mind-reading. Scalp EEG measures summed electrical activity, not thoughts. Emotion or mental-state inference is limited and context-dependent; results can vary across tasks, people, devices, and settings. [9]

Clinical boundary. AI models may assist research, screening, or clinician workflows. They are not standalone diagnosis or treatment, and performance in a study does not guarantee benefit for an individual.

03 / A neuron in silico

Hodgkin–Huxley with Brian2

Cₘ · dV/dt = I − gNa·m³h(V−ENa) − gK·n⁴(V−EK) − gL(V−EL)
m, h, n: voltage-dependent channel gates

  1. Write the membrane equation and gate dynamics; use consistent Brian2 units. [6, 7]
  2. Define a one-cell NeuronGroup with the HH state variables and parameters.
  3. Inject a current pulse, then run the simulation for a short interval.
  4. Record voltage with StateMonitor; plot spikes and vary the input current.

This is a model workflow: spikes depend on chosen equations, parameters, and numerical settings. It illustrates membrane dynamics; it is not a measurement from a person.
